A tool I could build myself

I wanted a low-cost way to make circuit boards for my own projects. Building the mill became a project in its own right: mechanical design, fabrication, and learning what small-scale manufacturing could realistically do.

The first version taught me about rigidity

My first design used inexpensive plated rods and resin-printed linear bearings. Friction, poor stability, and limited rigidity made it unsuitable for accurate PCB work. Subsequent versions improved the machine enough to produce boards for my electronics projects.

Useful capability, real limits

The mill produced double-sided copper boards for the e-bike project. It also made the limits of my process clear: I could mill traces, but I could not add solder mask or reproduce all the features of professionally manufactured boards. Dense assemblies eventually justified outsourcing PCB fabrication.

Why this one matters

Making my own manufacturing equipment changed how I design parts. I had to understand the relationship between accuracy, stiffness, cost, and assembly, then carry those lessons into the projects the machine enabled.
